# Whether it is permissible to renounce social affairs?

## Bibliography

<!-- 

Notes:

It seems to answer this question it is useful:
(1) to specify what renunciation and social affairs are
(2) to have some criteria by which to measure permissibility
(3) to make the relevant distinctions and qualifications to address the different permutations of renouncing social affairs

In asking this question, I have in mind particularly the affairs, business, and duties of a citizen or of any person in relation to his fellow man in regard to politics, whether there is a recognized state, etc., or not. The "politics" of the sāsana or a given monastery are a different matter. Moreover, this doesn't include the general affair of seeking the welfare of all living beings, which presumably applies to even the most solitary and withdrawn hermit; only affairs as concern politics, the state, government, voting, elections, campaigning, legislature, lawgiving, etc. Thus, a monk might engage in any political affair, but still teach others or produce scholarly works and thereby improve the human lot, putting aside the question of the benefit of his abandonment of the defilements, example, etc.
It would be useful to have some shorthand to distinguish between the absolute and general welfare of living beings and political welfare and needs. 
Renunciation here need not only mean total withdrawl from any political affairs, but could also refer to specific acts of such renunciation, such as not voting, engaging in advocacy, etc.
I think permissibility here is broadly moral.
From the outset, I would qualify any permissibility claim as not applying to cases of necessity, like Aquinas below. (One might not think there is or could be a necessity for engagement in political affairs.) However, generally, under ordinary circumstances, especially in the case of the monk, can it be permissible? Some relevant factors below:
(1) Necessity
(2) Purpose
(3) Office

Relevant questions are:
(1) Whether political affairs are a distraction to the holy life? Positively detrimental?
(2) Whether renunciation of political affairs when one could make a positive difference is permissible?
(3) Whether politicsl affairs improve the welfare of living beings, or can?
(4) What goods outside of political goods does one living a withdrawn contemplative life provide?

Working backwards, if we conclude that political affairs are at best orthogonal to the holy life, if not antithetical or detrimental to it, and they are in some sense mutually exclusive, or enough so, then the question is how to evaluate each to determine which course to take. If political affairs would more greatly improve human welfare, would renunciation be permissible?

The preceding analysis seems to work from further back. Another question is simply what is warranted by the EBTs.

Here is one line of thinking one might take with little regard for rigor. It is not intended to represent my view, which is undecided.

It seems to me that no one is exempt from renouncing the value of the true welfare of all living beings. Moreover, neither is anyone exempt from renouncing the active pursuit of that end in some form or another. However, I understand social affairs to be distinct from this end of the welfare of all beings and at most a means to it; and thus, it is possible to pursue the aforementioned end without involvement in social affairs, such as through teaching the Dhamma or making available resources pertaining to the Dhamma.
The true welfare of all beings is envisioned in Nibbāna, remainderless freedom from all stress and suffering. Disagreements about the precise nature of this attainment aside, the remainderless exhaustion of all defilements is considered inseparable from this end. This, however, in some cases, might require or at least be served by withdrawal from certain activities, such as social affairs, that have no inherent or immediate bearing on the training of one's own mind for the attainment of Nibbāna, or the basic prerequisites for that pursuit to occur, or the like pursuit of one's fellows.
Insofar as there is no need, then, one seeking the welfare of all beings is permitted to renounce social affairs for the purpose of refining the conditions for their attainment of the end of all suffering.

The question, though simple, still troubles me at this point. I do not yet have a clear enough picture to clearly define even what an answer might be, as so many issues weigh on this one question.

"Reply to Objection 3: To neglect human affairs when necessity forbids is wicked; otherwise it is virtuous. Hence Cicero says a little earlier: "Perhaps one should make allowances for those who by reason of their exceptional talents have devoted themselves to learning; as also to those who have retired from public life on account of failing health, or for some other yet weightier motive; when such men yielded to others the power and renown of authority." This agrees with what Augustine says (De Civ. Dei xix, 19): "The love of truth demands a hollowed leisure; charity necessitates good works. If no one lays this burden on us we may devote ourselves to the study and contemplation of truth; but if the burden is laid on us it is to be taken up under the pressure of charity."
