# Whether the Buddha ever disparaged someone for asking a question?

## Bibliography

<!-- 

Notes:

I recall with high confidence that someone in a <i>Skill in Questions<i> class asked a question to this effect. Taan Ajaan’s answer: no, or he couldn’t think of anything, or something like that. I do not have a settled view. Nonetheless, the following excerpts from the Canon are what first came to mind:

“atha kho aññatarassa bhikkhuno evaṃ cetaso parivitakko udapādi – 

"iti kira, bho, rūpaṃ anattā, vedanā anattā, saññā anattā, saṅkhārā anattā, viññāṇaṃ anattā, anattakatāni kammāni kamattānaṃ phusissantī"ti? 

atha kho bhagavā tassa bhikkhuno cetasā cetoparivitakkamaññāya bhikkhū āmantesi – 

"ṭhānaṃ kho panetaṃ, bhikkhave, vijjati yaṃ idhekacco moghapuriso avidvā avijjāgato taṇhādhipateyyena cetasā satthu sāsanaṃ atidhāvitabbaṃ maññeyya – 
‘iti kira, bho, rūpaṃ anattā, vedanā anattā, saññā anattā, saṅkhārā anattā, viññāṇaṃ anattā, anattakatāni kammāni kamattānaṃ phusissantī’ti. paṭivinītā kho me tumhe, bhikkhave, tatra tatra dhammesu". (MN 109)

ekamantaṃ nisinnā kho, bhante, migasālā upāsikā maṃ etadavoca – 

‘kathaṃ kathaṃ nāmāyaṃ, bhante ānanda, bhagavatā dhammo desito aññeyyo, yatra hi nāma brahmacārī ca abrahmacārī ca ubho samasamagatikā bhavissanti abhisamparāyaṃ. pitā me, bhante, purāṇo brahmacārī ahosi ārācārī virato methunā gāmadhammā. so kālaṅkato bhagavatā byākato sakadāgāmipatto tusitaṃ kāyaṃ upapannoti. petteyyopi me, bhante, isidatto abrahmacārī ahosi sadārasantuṭṭho. sopi kālaṅkato bhagavatā byākato sakadāgāmipatto tusitaṃ kāyaṃ upapannoti. kathaṃ kathaṃ nāmāyaṃ, bhante ānanda, bhagavatā dhammo desito aññeyyo, yatra hi nāma brahmacārī ca abrahmacārī ca ubho samasamagatikā bhavissanti abhisamparāya’nti? 

evaṃ vutte ahaṃ, bhante, migasālaṃ upāsikaṃ etadavocaṃ – 

‘evaṃ kho panetaṃ, bhagini, bhagavatā byākata"’nti.

"kā cānanda, migasālā upāsikā bālā abyattā ammakā ammakasaññā, ke ca purisapuggalaparopariyañāṇe?” (AN 6.44, AN 10.75)

The question which I first ask is as follows: what are or would be the criteria necessary to fulfill for this question to be answered? Specifically, what counts as a question, and what as ridicule? Are there other considerations beyond the wording of the question that must be accounted for? It might seem so obvious what a question is so as to not warrant questioning as much. However, it might seem relevant to some to consider that the Canon is heavily standardized and redacted; and, moreover, that information relevant to this question could have been lost in the process of standardization and so forth. For instance, while the characters in both quotes are quoted as having posed questions, could it be that they actually made statements that were subsequently rendered as questions in the redaction process? Moreover, some, including the Canon itself (e.g., AN 4.42), distinguish between different kinds of questions. In English, for example, a common kind of question is a rhetorical question. In some cases, such a question is a question insofar as syntax is concerned, but might actually serve a declarative purpose. One could read both of the questions in the above quotes in this light, in which case, again, they are only syntactically questions, but functionally statements. And, further, one might think that this nullifies said quotes, since they don’t actually concern functionally questions but, say, beliefs and reductios posed as questions. Moreover, the intent and motive of the inquirer might seem relevant. Another concern is that questions can be complex, in the sense that they can contain presumptions that might be false. If, in posing a complex question, the presumptions therein are counted as assertions, then a question could contain both a declaration and an inquiry. In such a case, one would be left to discern precisely which part, the declaration or the inquiry, the critic (e.g., the Buddha), is criticizing.

This remains left to be examined and resolved. However, if we assume that the questions asked in the quoted discourses are questions in the relevant sense of this article and that they actually represent what was spoken by the original inquirers, what is left to consider is the verb “disparaged”. It should be noted that the choice of verb here is not so much determining the question, but rather the question is determining the verb. There are different gradations and modes of being harsh, critical, mean, etc., to another, and different verbs encode those different gradations and modes. The question is aimed at asking whether or not the Buddha engaged in one such action. Thus, one could think here that the Buddha doesn’t engage in mockery, recrimination, but nonetheless it might still seem there is some other action happening worthy of interest. So, if disparagement is not the most apt term, then one might think there must be another which can do justice to the spirit of the question. 

On the iOS version of Merriam Webster, one definition of disparagement is:

“to speak slightingly about (someone or something) : to belittle the importance or value of (someone or something)”

At face value, one might think this is more or less how the Buddha spoke to and about the people in the above quotes.

Thus, again, leaving many details unexamined and determined, assuming the two aforesaid assumptions, and further that the Buddha did speak disparagingly in the presented quotes, the Buddha did, at least twice, disparage someone for asking a question. Perhaps the more interesting question is whether he ever did so to someone asking a sincere question. One very well could interpret both of the above quotes as depicting people who were insincere in their inquiries—perhaps aiming to laud themselves and degrade another. Moreover, one might think in such cases it could actually be suitable to speak disparagingly (I am non-committal here, though I tend to incline away from such a view). Yet, what about times when the inquirers seem rather sincere? 

The first passage for consideration that comes to mind is MN 136:

“sace pana [kiṃ pana (ka.)], bhante, āyasmatā samiddhinā idaṃ sandhāya bhāsitaṃ – yaṃ kiñci vedayitaṃ taṃ dukkhasmi"nti.

atha kho [evaṃ vutte (syā. kaṃ.)] bhagavā āyasmantaṃ ānandaṃ āmantesi – "passasi no tvaṃ, ānanda, imassa udāyissa moghapurisassa ummaṅgaṃ [ummaggaṃ (sī. syā. kaṃ. pī.), umaṅgaṃ (ka.)]? aññāsiṃ kho ahaṃ, ānanda – ‘idānevāyaṃ udāyī moghapuriso ummujjamāno ayoniso ummujjissatī’ti (MN 136)

"'But, venerable sir, supposing when the venerable Samiddhi spoke, he was referring to this, namely, 'Whatever is felt is suffering.'" 

Then the Blessed One addressed the venerable Ananda: "See, Ananda, how this misguided man Udayin interferes. I knew, Ananda, that this misguided man Udayin would unreasonably interfere now.” (Ñāṇamoli)

"But what if Ven. Samiddhi was speaking in reference to this: 'Whatever is felt comes under stress'?"

When this was said, the Blessed One said to Ven. Ānanda, "Look, Ānanda, at how this worthless Udāyin interrupts. I knew just now that he would interrupt in an inappropriate way.” (Ṭhānissaro)

The issue is, following Be, whether this is a question either syntactically or functionally. Evidently, as seen in the quote, the Cambodian edition would seem to clearly contain a question. The Buddha, in describing the Udāyī’s utterance, uses the word “ummaṅga”, defined in DPD on TPR as a “good question; auspicious enquiry; (comm) wise investigation”. Ñāṇamoli does not have a syntactic question, but Taan Ajaan does. In terms of function? Both, however, seem to agree in rendering “ummaṅga” not as a question but as an interjection.

The commentary has:

taṃ dukkhasminti sabbaṃ taṃ dukkhameva. iti imaṃ vaṭṭadukkhaṃ kilesadukkhaṃ saṅkhāradukkhaṃ sandhāya "sace bhāsitaṃ bhaveyya bhagavā"ti pucchati.
ummaṅganti pañhāummaṅgaṃ. ummujjamānoti sīsaṃ nīharamāno. ayoniso ummujjissatīti anupāyena sīsaṃ nīharissati. idañca pana bhagavā jānanto neva dibbacakkhunā na cetopariyañāṇena na sabbaññutañāṇena jāni, adhippāyeneva pana aññāsi. kathentassa hi adhippāyo nāma suvijāno hoti, kathetukāmo gīvaṃ paggaṇhāti, hanukaṃ cāleti, mukhamassa phandati, sannisīdituṃ na sakkoti. bhagavā tassa taṃ ākāraṃ disvā "ayaṃ udāyī sannisīdituṃ na sakkoti, yaṃ abhūtaṃ, tadeva kathessatī"ti oloketvāva aññāsi

[Tentatively]

“That is [included] in suffering.”: all that is just suffering. Thus, concerning the suffering of the round [of saṃsāra], the suffering of defilement, the suffering of formations, he asks “If it were stated, Blessed One”….

“Rising”: stretching out the head. “He will interject inappropriately”: he will stretch out the head improperly. And this, however, the Blessed One knew not from the divine eye, nor knowledge encompassing others’ minds, nor the knowledge of omniscience, but he knew it just by the inclination [of Udāyī], as the inclination of one wishing to speak is quite easily known. One wishing to speak stretches the neck forth, moves the jaw, shakes the mouth, and can’t sit still. The Blessed One, seeing that behavior of his and reflecting, knew “This Udāyī can’t sit still. He will say only what is false”.

Translation aside, it seems the commentary at least reads Udāyī’s utterance as a question ("pañhāummaṅgaṃ").
