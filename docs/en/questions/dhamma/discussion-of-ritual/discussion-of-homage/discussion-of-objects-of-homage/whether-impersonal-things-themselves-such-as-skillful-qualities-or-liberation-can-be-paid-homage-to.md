# Whether impersonal things themselves, such as skillful qualities or liberation, can be paid homage to?

## Bibliography

<!-- 

Notes:

AN 7.33: "satthugaru dhammagaru, saṅghe ca tibbagāravo, samādhigaru ātāpī". "One with respect for the teacher and Dhamma, and strong respect for the Saṅgha, one who is ardent with respect for concentration."

The Mora Paritta: "Namo bodhiyā...namo vimuttiyā". Is there not also the case of the Buddha paying homage to the Dhamma after the awakening?

The above could be taken to suggest that inanimate qualities can be the subject of at least respect, in the case of AN 7.33, or even salutation, in the case of the Mora Paritta, and if AN 7.33 is construed as implying salutation, beyond just respect. AN 7.33 could just be interpreted as saying that one should have respect for concentration in the sense of devotion; and further, devotion here is not devotion in a reverential, salutational, or venerable sense, but in the sense of striving, effort, steadfastness, and so on, as when it is said, "He is devoted to his profession." The aim of this interpretation is to circumvent the oddity of respecting something which isn't a person, by viewing "respect" in an honorary or analogical sense. The trouble for this interpretation is that in the Mora Paritta one would expect a univocal usage of "namo". Assuming "namo" in "namatthu Buddhānaṃ" means homage in the ordinary sense, it would be expected that such is its meaning in the quotes above regarding awakening and liberation. 

It is also worth considering, what precisely is the object being discussed here. For instance, while a human being, without ontological commitment to a self or such, is at least seemingly a more concrete entity. Even if one adopts an ontological view of Nibbāna, is vimutti a concrete thing in its own right that can be saluted? Whose vimutti? Perhaps a more plausible view is that suggested for AN 7.33 above. Or perhaps the object could be taken to be vimutti in the sense of an abstract object, perhaps a type or universal. This might entail more ontological commitment than desirable, so maybe the object is just vimutti as an abstract (in the non-philosophical sense) idea, as when it is said, "He respects the law", or "He fights for justice."

What could also be suggested is a functional interpretation, whereby one respects and pays homage to these qualities by behaving in such a way as to bring them about in themselves. Thus, when one practices the factors conducive to concentration, for instance, and practices concentration itself, that is the act of respect or homage. At present, this is distinct from the first view, wherein a different sense of the word "respect" is being employed, meaning something like "devotion", "diligence", and so on. Here, respect is understood in the normal sense, but what that act is is taken to be behaving in the right way in relation to the object of respect. Or perhaps this is more a "personal" interpretation. Respect isn't paid to some distant abstract object, but rather to the development of these qualities as they manifest internally. The objectual interpretation seems to entail some single object which all can pay respect to, but difficulties arise when considering how to generalize something like liberation, which seems to pertain to individual minds. However, on the personal interpretation, one is respecting their individual liberation. It should be noted that this is not intended to entail that only the liberated can engage in such obeisances. This is where the functional aspect may be applied: the respect can consist in the proper conduct in relation to one's individual liberation; i.e., developing the path towards liberation. 

Nonetheless, the personal-cum-functional interpretation appears wanting, for it still presupposes an object of respect, the identity of which isn't clear. Even if the object is one's personal liberation, this question is raised: what is that object for one who is not liberated? The motivation for the personal-cum-functional interpretation was to bypass the ontological commitments of the objectual interpretation. If it is said that the object is one's potentials for liberation, which some may consider equally present in all beings, the question remains: what are those potentials?

Nonetheless, the question is not the identity of the object, but merely if the inanimate object can be paid homage to. While an account of what the object is might be sought, some would say it is not necessary to determine whether or not it can be the object of homage. For instance, some would maintain that it is an open question about what the object is when the Buddha is paid homage to, given that the Buddha, not even when living, could be identified with any aggregate, and was given to profundity and immeasurability; yet, they would also maintain that no one doubts that the Buddha can be paid homage to, as this is overwhelmingly the pre-philosophical intuition of most, as can be observed before nearly any Buddhist shrine.

Perhaps there is another issue with the personal interpretation. Suppose the object of one's homage is one's potential for liberation. It is unclear what relevant difference there would be between one's potential and another's, assuming all are qualified to pay homage to their own potential. Thus, would it follow that everyone would be worthy of homage, in the sense of worthy of everyone's homage? Perhaps this issue can be circumvented by stipulating that there are restrictions and degrees with respect to paying homage. Thus, a monk of ten years standing. may be worthy of homage by some people, such as lay people and monks junior to him, but it would not be suitable for a monk of twenty years standing to pay homage to him. Similarly, one might say that homage to one's potential for liberation permits of degrees and qualifications.

I recall a discussion with Dr. Craig, I believe, in which something along the following lines was asked of him: whether or not one can, does, and should worship God's properties, like omnipotence, omniscience, and so on? This would have to be confirmed, but I recall him saying no.

1. Analogical interpretation
2. Objectual interpretation
3. Personal interpretation
