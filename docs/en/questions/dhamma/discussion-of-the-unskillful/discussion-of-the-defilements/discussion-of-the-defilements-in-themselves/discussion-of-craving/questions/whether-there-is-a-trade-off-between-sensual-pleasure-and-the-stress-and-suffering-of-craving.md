# Whether there is a trade-off between sensual pleasure and the stress and suffering of craving?

## Bibliography

<!-- 

Notes:

This question is not concerned with the gratification of craving, which is treated elsewhere, but with the pleasure of sensual pleasures themselves, something which can to some extent remain once sensual craving is removed (e.g., the Buddha still enjoyed a pleasant natural vista). The question is, if we grant that an extensive, though not total, abstention from sensual pleasures themselves is necessary to abandon craving, could it be that the loss of that sensual pleasure tips the scales against the side of renunciation and the abandonment of craving? The renunciate response is surely "no". There are multiple ways to disarm this position. I don't have a decisive definition of what a "loss" is, but merely abstaining from one pleasure is certainly not sufficient. If I abstain from chocolate ice cream so that I can eat vanilla instead, since I only have the appetite for one scoop of either, there is no loss, ceteris paribus. The reason is that the pleasure abstained from is compensated for by some other good or pleasure. Thus, similarly, the fact that the renunciate abstains from sensual pleasures wouldn't seem to be a loss unless there was no compensatory good. But, obviously, on the renunciate view.

The way the question is posed is along these lines: sure, there is stress involved in craving, but there are costs in anything worth doing. Just like in order to start a business, one may have to go into debt, one has to make some sacrifices regarding sensuality for the greater pleasure it can offer.
