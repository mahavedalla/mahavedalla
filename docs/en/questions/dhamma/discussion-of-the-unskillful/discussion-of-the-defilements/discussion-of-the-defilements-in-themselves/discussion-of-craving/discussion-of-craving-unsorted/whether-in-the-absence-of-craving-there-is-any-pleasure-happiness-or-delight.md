# Whether in the absence of craving there is any pleasure, happiness, or delight?

## Bibliography

<!-- 

Notes:

Clarifying the Objection

The objection begins from the plausible observation that much of what living beings ordinarily take to be pleasure is bound up with craving and its satisfaction. Hunger seeks food, sexual desire seeks gratification, ambition seeks success, and in each case the satisfaction of craving is experienced as pleasurable. From this perspective, craving appears to be the primary engine of enjoyment in human life.

The objector grants that craving is often stressful and that its frustration produces suffering. However, they argue that the removal of craving, while eliminating this suffering, simultaneously eliminates the pleasure bound up with its gratification. If the felt enjoyment of life largely consists in craving being satisfied, then extinguishing craving threatens to leave little or no pleasure, happiness, or delight behind.

Importantly, this objection is not about the pleasant qualities of the objects of craving as such (e.g., tastes, sounds, or sights), but about the pleasure that accompanies the gratification of craving itself. The worry is that if craving is removed, the very mechanism by which pleasure is generated is lost, resulting in an affectively flat or impoverished life.

Renunciate Response

From the renunciate perspective, this objection rests on a misidentification of the pleasure involved in craving’s gratification. The pleasure that accompanies the satisfaction of craving is not a positive good added to experience, but a form of negative reinforcement—namely, the temporary relief of a stressful and agitating condition. For this reason, the pleasure of gratification presupposes the prior presence of craving as an affliction.

On this view, it is precisely in the absence of craving that genuine pleasure, happiness, and delight become possible, just as freedom from cold, illness, or pain is itself experienced as pleasant despite involving no corresponding act of gratification. The disappearance of the relief associated with craving is not a loss, because what is removed is the need for relief in the first place. Thus, the absence of craving does not entail the absence of pleasure, but rather the absence of a disturbance whose temporary alleviation was mistaken for enjoyment.

From the renunciate perspective, this reply does not require denying the ordinary valuation of sensual pleasures or assuming a commitment to radical renunciation at the outset. One need only accept the widely shared and independently plausible claim that craving is itself a stressful or agitating condition. Once this is granted, it follows that the pleasure accompanying the gratification of craving cannot be a positive good in its own right, but must consist in the temporary relief of that stress.

Thus, even those who reject extensive renunciation can agree that the pleasure of craving’s satisfaction is structurally negative reinforcement rather than an added benefit. Acknowledging this does not yet commit one to abandoning sensual pleasures altogether, but it does undermine the claim that craving itself is what makes life pleasant. The renunciate position simply presses this insight to its conclusion by asking whether it is rational to preserve a condition of stress for the sake of the relief it intermittently provides.
