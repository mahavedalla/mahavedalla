# Whether there are prerequisites for living in seclusion?

<!--

Notes:

I currently believe that there are. The question asked raises many additional questions, particularly about the kinds of prerequisites for seclusion. The primary focus of this question concerns the prerequisites directly connected with the training and path. Nonetheless, basic medical knowledge, navigational skills perhaps, suitable timing and location, and so on, all also seem worthy of consideration as prerequisites unique to the question of seclusion. For, while a suitable place is perhaps always or often a prerequisite for practice, one could think seclusion has further unique needs as far as location goes. For instance, being freer of natural dangers, such as animals, rotten trees, etc., that pose a greater danger to one who is alone, than one with a second, since one alone has no recourse to another for help. The same might apply to medical knowledge. One living secluded might also need heightened practical medical knowledge to quickly identify, treat or seek treatment for medical issues that will arise, since they might be without others near skilled to offer said support. While these particular examples might not be satisfactory, it seems plausible to me that seclusion will require more than strictly spiritual prerequisites.

The gradual training quoted in MN 27 does not, as far as I am aware, there or elsewhere explicitly state that the fulfillment of the previous factors of the training are prerequisites for seclusion. Nonetheless, on one interpretation it seems implied by the sequence of the factors that one is to seek seclusion once the preliminary factors have been fulfilled, at least to some extent. 

AN 5.114 is noteworthy as the Buddha is quoted as explicitly telling disciples to encourage new monks to live in the forests, which is then implicitly equated with bodily seclusion (kāyavūpakāsa). On one interpretation, this would seem to lower the standard of the prerequisites for seclusion, if new monks are not expected to be endowed with all of the prerequisites implied by the other discourses (relative freedom from anger and sensuality, purified virtue, contentment, etc.); and, this assumed that the discourse is generalizable to many or all new monks, and was not limited to a specific set thereof.

It is here that the distinctions in the kinds of seclusion might also be relevant. If the distinctions offered elsewhere (see question) are permitted, then the question is raised as to what kind of seclusion the Buddha speaks about in the quoted discourses and the kind(s) this question is concerned with. For instance, if seclusion in AN 5.114 refers to simply living in the wilderness in a monastery or hermitage (perhaps, according to the current scheme, this is spatial, but not interactional nor participational seclusion), then the effect of the sutta might seem reduced. On this interpretation, a new monk should live in a <i>secluded forest<i>, perhaps secluded from worldly people, but not secluded from one’s fellow monks and teachers and so on, such as in an solitary cabin in uninhabited forest free from communal ties, etc.
