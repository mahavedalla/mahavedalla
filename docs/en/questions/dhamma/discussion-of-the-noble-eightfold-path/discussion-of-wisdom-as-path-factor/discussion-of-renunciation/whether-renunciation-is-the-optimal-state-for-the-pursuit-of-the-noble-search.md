# Whether renunciation is the optimal state for the pursuit of the noble search?

## Bibliography

<!-- 

Notes:

This question seems to be more or less identical to this one, "Whether going forth is superior to the household life with respect to the pursuit of the noble search?", both being kept for now to discern which is better worded and more direct. The question arises for various reasons, primarily to explain precisely why renunciation, going forth, wandering in voluntary poverty, etc., is the ideal mode of life for the sake of the noble search. It would be useful to respond to the question in a way that also includes other monastic and ascetic traditions that are designed similarly for the realization of some aim, religious, soteriological, or such like, just simply not conceived in the same way. That, because it seems many if not most of the world's monastic traditions are in striking agreement about the basic pillars of the lifestyle of the monk: poverty, chastity, submission to a teacher and rule, solitude, renunciation of sensual pleasures and worldly values, etc.

The Canon very concisely, candidly, and honestly captures the optimality and superiority of the renunciate state in the common refrain accompanying the decisiion to go forth of many: 

>Sambādho gharāvāso rajopatha, abbhokāso pabbajjā. Nayidaṃ sukaraṃ agāraṃ ajjhāvasatā ekantaparipuṇṇaṃ ekantaparisuddhaṃ saṅkhalikhitaṃ brahmacariyaṃ carituṃ. Yaṃnūnāhaṃ, kesamassuṃ ohāretvā, kāsāyāni vatthāni acchādetvā, agārasmā anagāriyaṃ pabbajeyyaṃ?

This can be spelled out, however. It seems the household life is crowded and dusty because it is generally involves large amounts of duties and business unconnected with the noble search. Such duties and business require both time and energy, detracting from the time and energy that can be used for the development of the path. Moreover, such duties and business tend to be of such a kind as are very distracting to the mind.

Beyond this, the household life is a life in the world, and thus exposed to worldly values. But said values at best are orthogonal to those of the path, though often antithetical thereto; and thus, to protect their values, it seems suitable for the seeker to leave behind the household for a more conducive environment in a monastery or in solitude, where there is less risk of the atrophy of their skillful values or even a total fall. This seems to raise interesting questions about what exactly going forth is and what the household life is. To provide the latter reason for the optimality of renunciation rests on renunciation involving physical or spiritual distance from the world, yet many Buddhist renunciates very much live in the world, such as monks of ecclesiastical rank, professors, some teachers, and so on. 

Considering some objections to renunciation. Perhaps one could think that renunciation, since it involves voluntarily letting go of wealth and property, limits one's material freedom; yet, since one is better of with more material freedom than less so they may procure whatever it might be they need for the purposes of the path, it is better not to renounce. This is contrived. The controversial premise is the second one. It seems every monastic tradition is going to deny that material freedom is a gain.
