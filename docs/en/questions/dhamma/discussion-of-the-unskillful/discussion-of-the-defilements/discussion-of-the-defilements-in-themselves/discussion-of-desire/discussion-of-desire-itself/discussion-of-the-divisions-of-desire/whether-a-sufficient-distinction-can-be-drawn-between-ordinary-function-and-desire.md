# Whether a sufficient distinction can be drawn between ordinary function and desire?

## Bibliography

<!-- 

Notes:

An interesting illustration that there is a distinction is at MN 75. The Buddha heavily critiques sensuality "kāma", yet in the same discourse also describes someone freed from sensuality as "yena kāmaṃ gamo", which I render as "going wherever he desires".

Also at MN 39, there is "sāmaññatthikāna", which means desiring monkhood. I have not found a corresponding usage for a cognate of "chanda" in ordinary parlance, thus excluding such passages as "chandaṃ janeti", etc., pertaining specifically to spiritual discipline. (Having read on in MN 39), there is "nacchādeyya", derived from "chādeti", which is the cognate verb of "chanda", according to the DPD. Moreover, it is used in an ordinary context; in this case to refer to some for whom food might (hypothetically) not agree, does not "chadeyya". This same discourse also contains "yenakāmaṃgamo".

At SN 54.12: [The Buddha] "icchāmahaṃ, bhikkhave, temāsaṃ paṭisallīyituṃ." These suttas don't precisely suit this question, but rather the question of whether their is a distinction between kilesachanda and desire qua functional initiative implicit in the early discourses. These discourses seem to clearly highlight that the critique of craving, lust, passion, and desire found in the early discourses is not intended to extend to the whole range of words like "wish" and "desire", either in Pāḷi or in English, which would seem to have problematic consequences.
