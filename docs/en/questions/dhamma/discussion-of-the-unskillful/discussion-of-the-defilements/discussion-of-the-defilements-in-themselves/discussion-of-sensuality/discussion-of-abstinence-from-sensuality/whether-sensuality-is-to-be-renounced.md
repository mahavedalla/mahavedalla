# Whether sensuality is to be renounced?

## Bibliography

<!-- 

Notes:

This is intended to be a question consummating a long series of previously addressed questions regarding sensuality and craving. This question is intended to touch on the essential theme of the rationality of renunciation. At this stage, it is not intended to introduce any new information, but simply to tie together various other questions into a coherent and holistic statement about renunciation. At present, the question deliberately appeals to "sensuality" as a whole, not sensual pleasures, or the gratification of sensual craving, specifically.

Beginning...

The first and most obvious objection to renunciation is that sensuality is pleasant and, to some, extremely pleasant, among the great goods of life. There are various ways to reply. Proceeding in one, the pleasure of sensuality must be distinguished into the gratification of craving and raw sensual pleasure. At present, I could proceed by stating that the former, as already argued, is a maladaptive remedial pleasure, and therefore, far from constituting a good which could exercise influence in a rational trade off with renunciation, is an ill and must be renounced. Appeal should probably also be made to the various other drawbacks of craving, such as those moral and practical. Regarding the former pleasure—raw sensual pleasure—at present, there are a couple of ways to proceed. One, by conceding that sensual pleasure is a pleasure of some weight in a rational trade off with renunciation; two, by admitting it is a pleasure, but one of no weight in said trade off. The latter is a stronger and more radical claim, and thus more controversial. At present it seems more suitable to concede that raw sensual pleasure is a pleasure that does have to be accounted for in a trade-off with renunciation, but to maintain that, nonetheless, when the numbers are crunched, that pleasure is far outweighed by other goods and pleasures. Of course, both of the former points could be objected to here. It seems the able defender of sensuality would not attempt a rebuttal of the former charge, but question the relative weights of raw sensual pleasures and competing renunciate goods. They would perhaps also proceed to the second objection.

The second objection makes an notable move by introducing other considerations besides pleasure into the discussion of the rationality of renunciation: namely, it contends that sensuality does not merely carry weight for its hedonic prospects, but its value and meaningfulness, its axiological prospects. That is to say, is valuable in itself, and adds value and meaning to the lives of living beings: romantic relationships are a good unto themselves, that also confer on their members weighty goods like duty, meaning, fulfillment, purpose, and so on. How does the renunciate respond? As will have been argued elsewhere, it seems the renunciate doesn't need to deny the claim holistically, but merely counter that however valuable sensuality is, it is outweighed by other renunciate goods. Nonetheless, a steadfast renunciate would seem to largely reject the objection, denying that sensuality, either intrinsically or extrinsically, has much value. Perhaps said renunciate would go a step further and assert the positive disvalue of sensuality.

Objection three could proceed from the claim that sensual craving is an instrumental and necessary part in the production of weighty goods; and, therefore, since the renunciation defended herein is aimed at abandoning that very craving, it poses a grave dangers to those goods. Goods here could refer to such goods as art, literature, architecture, science, the economy, philosophy, and so on. Here as elsewhere, the reply could consist in a very strong claim denying any goodness to said goods, or a weaker claim conceding their goodness, but reaffirming the superior goodness of the goods connected with renunciation. In either case, the reply could also deny the instrumentality and necessity of craving to the production of at least some of the goods appealed to.
