# Whether there is a trade-off between sensual pleasure and the stress and suffering of craving?

## Bibliography

<!-- 

Notes:

This question is not concerned with the gratification of craving, which is treated elsewhere, but with the pleasure of sensual pleasures themselves, something which can to some extent remain once sensual craving is removed (e.g., the Buddha still enjoyed a pleasant natural vista). The question is, if we grant that an extensive, though not total, abstention from sensual pleasures themselves is necessary to abandon craving, could it be that the loss of that sensual pleasure tips the scales against the side of renunciation and the abandonment of craving? 

The renunciate response is surely "no". I don't have a decisive definition of what a "loss" is, but merely abstaining from one pleasure is certainly not sufficient. If I abstain from chocolate ice cream so that I can eat vanilla instead, since I only have the appetite for one scoop of either, there is no loss, ceteris paribus. The reason is that the pleasure abstained from is compensated for by some other good or pleasure. Thus, similarly, the fact that the renunciate abstains from sensual pleasures wouldn't seem to be a loss unless there was no compensatory good. But, obviously, on the renunciate view, there are compensatory goods. To suggest otherwise would seem to beg the question.

The way the question is posed is along these lines: sure, there is stress involved in craving, but there are costs in anything worth doing. Just like in order to start a business, one may have to go into debt, one has to make some sacrifices regarding sensuality for the greater pleasure it can offer. 

The issue is one of framing. The renunciate doesn't wish to say that we shouldn't take risks for the sake of greater goods. The renunciate sees craving as a disease, as in the image of a leper, an affliction, on account of which there are no greater pleasures being accrued. 

There are multiple ways to disarm this position. The first consists in denying the weight of raw sensual pleasure (grass and twigs). The second goes further thant the first by even maintaining that certain sensual activities taken to be pleasant are actually unpleasant. The third simply maintains that there are other goods and pleasures not connected with sensual pleasures. The fourth maintains that the raw pleasure of sensuality is simply of no consequence to someone without craving therefore. 

Firstly, the robust renunciate position found in the early discourses is that raw sensual pleasure is insignificant, both hedonically and perhaps axiologically. This is to say that in the absence of craving and the pleasure of its gratification, the raw sensual pleasure that remains of sensuality is not that pleasant, as perhaps neither is a meal to a full stomach; and, further, that that remaining pleasure is of trifling value. 

Secondly, the aforesaid renunciate position makes a surprising manuever and maintains that, contrary to popular opinion, at least some sensual experiences which are perceived as pleasant are actually unpleasant, and so perceived by one whose sense faculties are not perverted by craving. It is uncertain if the Buddha ever explicitly states what those pleasures are, though perhaps a reasonable guess is that they are carnal bodily pleasures, such as sexual pleasures. The latter specifically is intimated AN 7.49 where the learned monk's reaction to the sexual act is akin to that of the withering of a feather from a flame. There is also AN 6.42 in which the Buddha refers to the pleasure of fame, though perhaps even all sensual pleasures as an excrement pleasure.

Thirdly, the renunciate is well advised here to make recourse to the goods and pleasures connected with renunciation. At AN 2.66, the position is stated clearly and concisely:

>Dvemāni, bhikkhave, sukhāni. katamāni dve? kāmasukhañca nekkhammasukhañca. imāni kho, bhikkhave, dve sukhāni. etadaggaṃ, bhikkhave, imesaṃ dvinnaṃ sukhānaṃ yadidaṃ nekkhammasukhaṃ.

And also:

>ye kho, ānanda, evaṃ vadeyyuṃ – ‘etaṃparamaṃ santaṃ sukhaṃ somanassaṃ paṭisaṃvedentī’ti – idaṃ nesāhaṃ nānujānāmi. taṃ kissa hetu? atthānanda, etamhā sukhā aññaṃ sukhaṃ abhikkantatarañca paṇītatarañca. (SN 36.19)

That is: the pleasure of renunciation is the superior pleasure. In other places, the phrase "pleasure of renunciation" seems to refer specifically to the four jhānas and the formless attainments. However, I am inclined to understand the pleasure of renunciation in the context of this article as being much more inclusive: all of the pleasures connected with developing the noble eightfold: the pleasure of virtue and discernment, the pleasure of solitude, or simplicity, of contentment, and so on. Then again, it is also at least partly true that all of the pleasures just listed are to some extent available to one still enjoying sensual pleasures. To avoid proposing a false trade-off, perhaps the additional claim must be presented that while the pleasures of renunciation are partly available to one enjoying sensual pleasures, they are not totally so; and, moreover, they are much harder to develop with sensual pleasures unrenounced, and far less stable under said conditions as well. In this way, renunciation and sensual pleasures are placed in competition with each other.

There is the question about whether or not any of the aforesaid comments are relevant to the question as stated. To argue that sensual pleasures is inferior to those of renunciation, and so on, is not to say that sensual pleasures do not carry some weight, merely that they are outweighed.
