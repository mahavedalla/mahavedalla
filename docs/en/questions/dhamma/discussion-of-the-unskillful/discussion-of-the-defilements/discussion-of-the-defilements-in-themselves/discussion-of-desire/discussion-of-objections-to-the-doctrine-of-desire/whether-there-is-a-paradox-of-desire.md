# Whether there is a paradox of desire?

## Bibliography

<!-- 

Notes:

I find that even the question is difficult to formulate and spell out clearly when it is inspected analytically. 

For instance, what kind of paradox is the precise one of interest here. I am mostly interested in logical ones (A&~A), since a logical paradox in the doctrine of desire would seem to be a fatal blow. If, however, non-logical paradoxes of interest arise, I would consider those. 

The exchange between Herman and Alt is perhaps a good foundation, followed by the later comments on said exchange by Webster, which I am yet to read in full, and the former two being read a time ago. 

Perhaps one important concern is temporal modality, which I recall with some confidence finding lacking in the exchange between Herman and Alt. Another concern is of scope, which I am struggling to specify in writing. For instance, is the question approached regarding the end of all desires considered holistically, or individual desires.

For instance:

In order to abandon any desire, one must desire to do so.

In order to abandon all desire, one must desire to do so.

Another matter is distinction in desire, which I recall with some confidence Webster mentioning. That is, which desire precisely is the target of this question, for one might there are relevant distinctions within the term "desire" that, if blurred, negatively impact this discussion.

Thus, one could think that skillful desires are not included among the desires to be abandoned, and further that the desire to abandon desire (abandonable desires excluding those skillful) is skillful, and thus, so the thought, the whole paradox is circumvented from the start.

Approaching this with concern for logical paradoxes, the aim is to demomstrate the doctrine contains or implies A&~A for some proposition A. If one is found, what remains is to inspect the assumptions of the paradox to see if they are actually necessary commitments of the doctrine. This alone seems to help. For instance, using one premise one might use to deduce paradox:

In order to end all desire, one must desire to end all desire. 

Prima facie the premise seems suspect, yet given the strict logical standard, it is unclear this alone, without auxiliary premises can produce paradox. If so, then it seems the proponent of the doctrine of desire simply has to deny whatever auxiliary premises those might be. 

One can import various similes to try to illustrate why the seemingly suspect premise is perfectly logical itself, such as driving out one nail with another, trying to jump up by first bending one's legs downward, or trying to fly a rocket by adding a massive fuel chamber to weigh it down.

While these similes prima facie might be quite helpful, it is unclear if they are analagous to the paradox of desire. What is sought is an logically coherent example of the instructions to, say, stop doing something requiring that that very thing be done.

Some attempts (exusing my poor creativity):

Imagine a monkey swinging from branch to branch among trees. In order to stop swinging, they must swing down, as it were. One might be concerned this is not perfectly analogous because the monkey does not must needs swing down. The monkey could just let go and fall, or select many other possibile options. One might, however, further not be concerned because the paradox still ensues if it is merely an option that one end desire by desiring so. Of course, one might also think it can just be stipulated that that is the only option for the monkey, for the sake of argument; and, further, that clearly no paradox ensues. It is possible one thinks this is disanalagous because by swinging down the monkey is directly and immediately tending towards swinging Nibbāna (the perfect ending of all swinging), whereas in desiring the end of all desire, we are adding to our lot of desires. Again, one might think this issue can be bypassed by various routes. For instance, one could deny outright that one is instructed to add to their new desires, but rather to sublimate old ones. Or, one could simply modify the hypothetical so that the monkey has to actually swing further up, for some reason (perhaps there was an asymmetry between route up and down, as in when one falls into a deep hole that doesn't mean one can thereby get out), in order to swing down to terra firma.
