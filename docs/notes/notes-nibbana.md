A few claims made by Tan Ajaan as I recall them:

1. Nibbāna is outside of space and time
2. There is no causality outside of space and time
3. The fetters are cut following (unsure of the verb he used, which is relevant) the experience of Nibbāna

Meditations 11: "It’s that glimpse—the seeing—that cuts the feers."

4. The experience of cessation lasts a moment

Meditations 11: When those internal causes are ended, your connection with the world of the senses ends as well. At least it stops for the time being, and that’s what opens the possibility for an experience of the deathless."

5. The Deathless is a dimension in the mind that can be touched

Meditations 11: "Whereas there’s the example of the Buddha: It is possible to develop our potentials so that we can find something deathless inside—something outside space and time. at’s a potential within us."

Meditations 11: "that there is a deathless element that can be touched at the mind."

The quote thus far for 3 above is not itself problematic: the vision of the Deathless, not the Deathless itself, cuts the fetters. The question is raised: what causes the vision? The problem is to explain how there is some relation between the Deathless and the Deathless vision, avoiding falling into a view where one's vision thereof, is totally unrelated to the Deathless itself; yet, it seems one must also avoid, accepting the claim of the causal inertness of the Deathless, saying the Deathless is a causal link in the vision thereof, just as in ordinary experience, it would seem that the actual object of one, say, sight, is partly causally responsible for the sight, along with other factors, such as brain functioning, etc.

It is also still unclear precisely what Nibbāna is in Tan Ajaan's view.

"This experience of the goal—absolutely unlimited freedom, beyond classification and exclusive of all else—is termed the elemental nibbāna property with no ‘fuel’ remaining (anupādisesa-nibbāna-dhātu)."

"Here Ven. Udāyin compares the nibbāna property with fuel remaining—the state of being absolutely free from passion, aversion, & delusion—to a fire whose flames have died out, but whose embers are still glowing"

"Thus the completely free & unadulterated experience we have been discussing is that of nibbāna after death"

"In this extraordinary state of mental poise—neither pressed, forced, blocked, or suppressed—nibbāna in the present life is experienced as freedom from all perception dealing with the six sensory spheres & the dimensions of meditative absorption. Although one is conscious, and these dimensions are present, one does not partake of them.
On the level of ordinary sensory experience, however, nibbāna in the present life is experienced by the Worthy One as the passing away of passion, aversion, & delusion. This implies that these three states are analogous to fire; and as we saw in the Introduction, they are directly referred to as fires at various points in the Canon. On the surface, the notion of passion & aversion as fires hardly requires explanation, but in order to gain a fuller appreciation of the analogies that the Canon draws between fire on the one hand, and passion, aversion, & delusion on the other, we first need some background on the specifically Buddhist views on fire it contains."

He seems to often speak of the experience of Nibbāna. It is unclear if the experience is simply supposed to be Nibbāna itself, or if Nibbāna is, at least in a manner of speaking, the object of said experience, though not the experience itself.
