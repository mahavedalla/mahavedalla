# Whether the abandonment of craving is repressive?

## Bibliography

<!-- 

Notes:

The objection combines several lines of thought:

The gratification of craving constitutes much of the happiness of most living beings.

The abandonment of craving requires the renunciation of sensual pleasures, which may seem like a loss.

Craving is a natural or necessary part of living beings’ functioning, and thus removing it could be seen as interfering with what is essential or “healthy” for life.

Renunciate Response

(1) As argued elsewhere, the pleasure of craving’s gratification is merely relief-pleasure—the alleviation of an affliction—rather than a positive good in itself. Its removal is therefore not a loss, and higher pleasures, including the delight of freedom from craving, remain.

(2) As previously explained, the renunciation of sensual pleasures does not constitute a genuine deprivation. Raw pleasures that remain after craving is removed are minor or trivial (next to “grass and twigs”), and the renunciate gains superior pleasures connected with the path.

(3) Craving is not a natural or necessary part of life. It is a disease or dysfunction that generates stress, distorts perception, and drives unskillful action. One can distinguish craving from life functions, ordinary wants, functional initiatives, preferences, and wholesome desires: the latter remain intact and functional once craving is removed. Far from being essential, craving is a source of suffering and impairment.

Concise Response

Mere restraint or abandonment of a craving, desire, or impulse cannot be considered repressive, provided that the restraint does not prevent the attainment of a good that could not otherwise be obtained. Otherwise, ordinary acts of restraint—such as refusing an extra glass of wine or refraining from sexual misconduct—would themselves count as repressive, which is clearly implausible.

Moreover, the restraint of that which would lead to harm for oneself or others is a skillful and beneficial function. Since craving contributes both to one’s own suffering and to unskillful or harmful actions toward others, its abandonment is not repressive, but in fact skillful and conducive to genuine well-being.
