# Whether tranquility is necessary for entering jhāna?

## Bibliography

<!-- 

Notes:

Norbu

Question selected: Whether tranquility (passaddhi) is necessary for entering jhāna, or whether strong mindfulness alone can suffice for absorption?

Objections
1. It seems tranquility is not necessary. For in many places concentration (samādhi) is said to be established by mindfulness (sati) alone, as when the Buddha speaks of mindfulness of breathing leading to concentration (see AN references; I may be mistaken about the numbering). Therefore strong mindfulness suffices.
2. Further, the seven factors of awakening do not require all to be fully present for liberation on that occasion (SN46 series; please check). Hence passaddhi, being one factor, need not be necessary for jhāna either.
3. Moreover, the abandonment of the five hindrances is the necessary condition for jhāna (e.g., MN passages on the first jhāna; please verify). But the hindrances can be suppressed by directed effort and guarding the sense-doors, which are functions of mindfulness and energy (sati, viriya), not tranquility. Therefore tranquility is not necessary.
4. Again, tranquility follows joy (pīti) in the bojjhaṅga sequence (sati → dhamma-vicaya → viriya → pīti → passaddhi → samādhi → upekkhā). If tranquility were necessary for entering jhāna, joy would always have to precede jhāna. Yet some attain absorption via neutral clarity without pronounced joy. Therefore tranquility is not required.
5. Finally, some non-Buddhist contemplatives attain absorptions through sheer fixation on a sign (nimitta) by forceful attention. This shows strong mindfulness or concentration alone suffices without tranquility.

On the contrary
In the standard first jhāna formula, after the hindrances are abandoned, there is rapture and pleasure born of seclusion with applied and sustained thought; the body and mind grow malleable, wieldy, and peaceful. In the awakening-factor sequence, tranquility stands prior to samādhi as its condition (SN46.3; please check). Also, the Abhidhamma analyzes tranquility (passaddhi) as calming of mental factors and of consciousness, which counters agitation and restlessness that obstruct unification. Hence tranquility is required as the proximate condition for stable absorption.

I answer that
Tranquility (passaddhi) is necessary for entering jhāna according to two senses: materially as a quality in the mind at the time of absorption, and causally as a typical proximate condition for the unification of mind.

First, materially: Jhāna is characterized by collectedness free from the five hindrances. Among these, restlessness-and-worry (uddhacca-kukkucca) is directly opposed by pacification. If agitation remains, unification is not secure. The composure of the mental “body” (kāya, here the constellation of mental factors) and of consciousness (citta) must be softened, cooled, and made smooth. This is precisely passaddhi. Therefore, even when the meditator emphasizes mindfulness, the arising of absorption includes the presence of tranquility as part of the wholesome constellation that constitutes jhāna.

Second, causally: In the bojjhaṅga dynamics, joy’s energy, when balanced, settles into tranquility; tranquility then supports deeper steadiness that ripens into samādhi. While the exact temporal unfolding can be swift and non-linear, the functional order holds: a mind that is tense, coarse, or jittery cannot sustain the even pressure of attention required for absorption. Mindfulness and energy collect the mind; tranquility refines and smooths it so that collection becomes seamless. Thus tranquility is a necessary condition in the ordinary course of practice.

Yet, tranquility need not be separately cultivated as a distinct technique apart from mindfulness. It commonly emerges from the convergence of ethical restraint, skillful attention, and steady contact with a suitable object. In this sense one might say “mindfulness suffices,” provided that “mindfulness” is not taken narrowly as mere noting, but as the right mindfulness of the path, balanced with right effort and right collectedness, through which tranquility naturally appears and functions.

Reply to Objection 1
Texts that attribute concentration to mindfulness do not exclude tranquility; rather, they speak by principal cause. Right mindfulness, when sustained, suppresses the hindrances and conduces to calming. Thus mindfulness brings about tranquility, which in turn supports concentration. The sufficiency asserted is not exclusivity but primacy.

Reply to Objection 2
It is true that not every awakening factor must be maximally manifest at all times. However, for jhāna as absorption, a minimum of pacification is inherent. Even if passaddhi is not consciously highlighted, the mind at absorption is by nature tranquil. Hence necessity here concerns constitutive presence, not a separate contemplative emphasis.

Reply to Objection 3
Guarding the senses and energetic effort can check the hindrances, but without pacification the suppression is brittle. Restlessness subtly persists as vibration and unevenness. Jhāna requires not only the absence of obstruction but the presence of ease and pliancy. Tranquility completes the work begun by effort and mindfulness by smoothing residual agitation.

Reply to Objection 4
While pīti is not always exuberant, some degree of uplift or refreshment typically precedes the deep settling that matures into tranquility. Even in approaches marked by neutral clarity, the mind passes through a quiet joy that is gentle rather than flashy. The sequence denotes functions, not theatrical intensity. Thus tranquility remains the bridge to stable samādhi.

Reply to Objection 5
Forceful fixation may stabilize attention, but without tranquility it tends toward tension, rigidity, or imbalance, susceptible to disturbance and unfit for the Buddha’s jhāna characterized by ease (sukha) and malleability. The distinction between strained absorption and the Buddha’s collectedness is its pacified, wholesome tone oriented to dispassion (virāga). Therefore tranquility is required for jhāna in the dispensation.

Practical note
Balance the factors: let mindfulness steady the object, allow joy to brighten without excess, and then emphasize relaxation of effort in body and mind so that tranquility can pervade. From that softness, unification matures. Sources mentioned include SN46 on awakening factors and standard jhāna formulas in MN; I may be mistaken about exact citations and encourage checking.
